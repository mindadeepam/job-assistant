# Interview prep

When an interview turns up, the job changes from volume to depth. Keep the prep for each company in `me/prep/<company>/`, so it's there for the next round.

## Start

Ask:
- the date and time, the format (phone, video, onsite, take-home), and what the round covers, if the recruiter said;
- who they're meeting, if known;
- how much time they have each day until then.

Work backwards from the date. A round in two days gets the essentials; one in two weeks gets a proper plan.

## Research

- **The company:** what it sells and to whom, how it makes money, recent news and launches, and the team's product.
- **The interviewers,** if known: role, background, what they've written or spoken about. Public profiles only.
- **The process:** what people commonly report about this company's rounds. Treat it as a hint, not a script.

Write one page, `me/prep/<company>/company.md`, they can read on the way.

## From the JD to the questions

- Map each must-have to the topics it implies and the questions likely in each round (screening, technical, case or system design, behavioural, hiring manager).
- For each likely question, note which of their projects answers it best, from `me/master_resume.md`.
- Flag the gaps, the topics they'll probably be asked about but haven't done, and decide with them: learn enough to talk about it, or prepare an honest "here's how I'd approach it".

## Stories

Turn their main projects into short stories (situation, what they did, result, with the bank's numbers) for the common behavioural asks: a hard problem, a disagreement, a failure, leading without authority, a tight deadline. Six to eight stories usually cover most questions. Practise them out loud; written stories always run too long.

## Mock interviews

Run them in chat, or by voice if the platform has it.
- One question at a time. Wait for their full answer before saying anything.
- Ask follow-ups the way a real interviewer would: "why that choice?", "what if the data were ten times bigger?", "what would you do differently?".
- Afterwards, honest feedback: what landed, what was vague, what was missing, and a stronger version of the weakest answer.
- Keep a list of weak spots and come back to them in the next mock.

For technical rounds, include coding or problem-solving practice in the format they'll face (a shared editor, a whiteboard, a take-home), timed if the real one is.

## Study plan

When there's material to learn or revise, write `me/prep/<company>/plan.md`, sized to the days left and the time they have each day. Notes they can read on their phone, practice problems, and a mock near the end. Track what they've done in `data/log.md`.

Some users want study help beyond one interview: a plan for a role family, revision notes, practice every day. Build that with them the same way, in `me/prep/` or a new skill of their own.

## Logistics and questions

- The time zone, the link or address, what to have open, and a test of their setup for video calls.
- Three or four questions to ask the interviewers, specific to the team and role.

## Afterwards

- A short debrief in `data/log.md`: questions asked, what went well, what to improve. Add good new stories or numbers to the bank.
- A thank-you note draft, if the user wants one.
- Prep for the next round, using what this one revealed.

## Offers

- Compare offers side by side: base, variable, equity, benefits, location, role, growth, team.
- For negotiation, use real ranges: what they've been offered, what recruiters said, and public salary data they can check (levels.fyi, Glassdoor, or the sites common in their market). Never invent figures. Draft what to say; the conversation is theirs.
